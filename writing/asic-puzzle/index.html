Recovering a netlist from a GDSII mask, and solving the puzzle it implements

Notes on the Jane Street ASIC reverse-engineering puzzle, August 2026

The puzzle ships a finished mask and very little else: no netlist, no source, and no statement of what the chip is for. These notes describe how I recovered a 728-cell gate-level netlist from the layout, what convinced me the recovery was exact rather than merely plausible, and how the recovered circuit turned out to be a judge for a Star Battle puzzle. The string the chip prints once it is satisfied is (* TWO STARS *).

1.Extracting connectivity from the layout

Opening puzzle.gds in gdstk shows that the standard cell names were left intact. There are 81 structures, a top cell called puzzle, and 9,875 placements drawn from the sky130_fd_sc_hd library along with a handful of via macros. Nothing therefore has to be recognised at the transistor level, and the only real question is which cell pins are electrically joined to which wires.

I wrote a small extractor on top of the STRtree index in shapely. It applies two rules. Shapes on the same layer that intersect belong to the same net, and a cut joins whatever it overlaps on the layer immediately below and the layer immediately above. After the hierarchy is flattened, every metal polygon and every cut goes into a union-find structure. Pins are then located from the text labels that each cell carries on its own li1 geometry, with each point-in-polygon lookup restricted to the shapes belonging to that particular instance so that a neighbouring cell's pin cannot be picked up by mistake. Top-level ports come from the labels on met3.

My first version of the extractor bridged layers using the via cell references that the router places, which seemed like the natural reading of the file. That version quietly lost every RESET_B connection in the design. The reason is that the sky130 cells already contain an mcon cut sitting on each pin's label point, so the router connects to a pin simply by painting met1 over that cut, without instantiating a via macro at all. Bridging on cut geometry rather than on via instances fixes the problem, and it is a closer description of the physical structure anyway.

2.Checking the extraction

A netlist that is almost right is not much use here, because the likely failure mode is a netlist that looks entirely reasonable and computes the wrong function. I did not try to interpret the design until the extraction had been checked from two independent directions.

The first check uses the warm-up example in the repository, which ships both 04_final.gds and the 01_netlist.v that produced it. Running the extractor on that GDS returns 79 cells with a cell-type histogram matching the reference netlist exactly, and no net is left undriven or multiply driven. This is also the check that exposed the mcon problem, since sixteen resets came out dangling where the reference netlist ties them to rst_n.

The second check uses the sample waveform. Replaying all 312 clock edges of example_inputs.vcd through the recovered puzzle netlist reproduces O[7:0] and success at every edge with no mismatches, including both TRY AGAIN bursts.

Two weaker signals also pointed the right way. The combinational graph came out acyclic on the first attempt, and exactly one net in the whole design has no driver. Both are what one would expect from a correct reading of a synthesised netlist.

3.Cell models and simulation

Cell behaviour did not have to be guessed at, because SkyWater publishes a functional Verilog model for every cell in the library. Each model is a short list of gate primitives, so I downloaded all 67 and wrote a parser of about ninety lines that turns them into Python callables. That avoids deriving truth tables for cells such as o2bb2a or a21boi by hand, which is easy to get subtly backwards.

The first simulator was an interpreter that walked the netlist on every evaluation. It was correct but too slow to explore with, and a sweep of 122 runs failed to finish inside a two minute limit. I replaced it with a compiler: the gates are visited in topological order, one Python assignment is emitted per primitive, and the resulting source is exec'd into a function.

4.Identifying the blocks

Once simulation is cheap, the quickest route to understanding 92 flip-flops is to experiment on the circuit rather than to work through its algebra by hand.

The first technique was exhaustive cofactoring. No flip-flop's next-state cone depends on more than 13 signals, so each one can be enumerated completely, and slicing the resulting table by counter value makes the structure legible. This showed a counter that cycles modulo 11 together with a second counter that advances whenever the first wraps, so the chip reads eleven groups of eleven bits. It also showed a motif that recurs throughout the design: pairs of flip-flops arranged as saturating two-bit counters, each incrementing on I during one particular time slot.

The second technique settled the details. I loaded words containing a single set bit, swept that bit across all 121 positions, and recorded which flip-flops ended up different. Because the counters saturate rather than wrap, one star always flips the low bit of any counter watching it, so the set of positions that disturb a given counter is exactly the set of grid cells that counter is responsible for. Eleven of them turned out to watch columns. The other eleven watch irregular connected shapes that partition the grid exactly.

The rest of the design followed from the same measurements. A twelve-stage shift register provides taps at 1, 10, 11 and 12 cycles back, which on an eleven-wide grid are the cells to the left, above right, above and above left; these feed a flag that latches if two stars are ever adjacent. A separate two-bit counter tracks the current row and sets a second latching flag. An eight-bit ripple counter accumulates the total number of bits presented.

The README's hint that the circuit is physically arranged to hint at its function becomes useful at this point. Colouring each gate by the flip-flop it feeds shows a floorplan that more or less announces the answer: one straight chain of twelve cells, two vertical stacks of eleven near-identical slices, and a counter running modulo 11.

5.The acceptance condition

success is one register deep, and that register's cone depends on 57 signals. Enumerating it is out of the question, but probing it with structured assignments is straightforward. Setting all eleven column counters to one value, all eleven region counters to another, and sweeping the accumulated total leaves exactly one accepting combination.

6.Solving it and reading out the answer

With the constraints named the search is short. A row-by-row backtracker that places two non-adjacent stars in each row and prunes on the remaining column and region budgets finds the grid in Figure 1 and confirms that it is the only solution. Serialising that grid in row-major order gives the input word.

7.Easter eggs

7.1Morse code in a row below the die

Thirty-six placements of two anonymised cells, INTERNAL_3 and INTERNAL_7, contain no circuitry whatsoever, only a marker rectangle. They sit in a single row at y = −52.72 µm, which is outside the die boundary, and they are the only geometry anywhere in the file that lies outside it. That is why they are absent from the layout image in the README, and why you meet them only if you open the GDS yourself.

Their widths and spacings are exact multiples of 1.38 µm. A one-unit cell is a dot and a three-unit cell is a dash, while the gaps between them measure one, three or seven units, which are the standard Morse spacings for symbol, letter and word.

7.2A sentence hidden in the sample inputs

The two failed attempts in example_inputs.vcd are not arbitrary. Each one is eleven rows of eleven bits, and the last four bits of every row are zero. The leading seven bits are an ASCII character sent least significant bit first.

7.3Two further messages in the ROM

The character ROM in the output generator is addressed partly by the eight-bit star total rather than by success alone, and two totals are treated specially. I checked all 256 totals against three different load states, together with roughly a thousand random and length-varied inputs. There are four messages and no fifth.

7.4An impossible timestamp

The VCD is dated Sat Dec 31 23:59:60 2016. A sixtieth second exists only during a leap second, and one was in fact inserted at the end of 31 December 2016, which was indeed a Saturday.

8.Difficulties

  • The mcon problem took the longest to find and would have been invisible without the warm-up example to compare against. If there is one lesson in all of this, it is to build the comparison harness before trusting anything the extractor reports.
  • Symbolic simplification turned out to be the wrong instrument. I began by pushing each next-state function through SOPform in sympy, which never finished even one 13-variable cone. Enumerated cofactor tables and simulation probes answered the same questions in milliseconds. For this sort of work it is more useful to observe the circuit than to put its functions into canonical form.
  • The single undriven net feeds two gates inside the output generator, which is uncomfortable when the whole argument rests on the extraction being exact. I settled it by compiling the design twice, once with that net tied low and once tied high, and confirming that the output bytes were identical for all 256 totals. The net has no effect, so it is a synthesis artefact rather than a wire I had missed.
  • Only every second cell row is occupied, which broke my first attempt at connected-component analysis of the floorplan, because slices of the same block were never quite touching. Collapsing the empty rows fixed it.
